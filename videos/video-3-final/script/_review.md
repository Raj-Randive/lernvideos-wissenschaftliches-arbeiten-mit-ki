## English translation of the narration (so you can check the content)

1. **Title.** Hello and welcome to the third learning video of this course. The topic: research process and methodology. In the last session you searched for and selected literature on your research question. Now comes the next building block: the basic methodological approach. We go from the research cycle to the question of how you would proceed in your six-pager.
2. **Agenda.** Today's video introduces the basic paradigms of scientific research, the empirical research process, and some scientific methods.
3. **Quantitative vs. qualitative.** There are two basic paradigms (methodological orientations). On one hand quantitative research: the focus is on testing hypotheses and generalising; measurement is standardised and comparative. Falsification is key: an assumption holds until it can be refuted. On the other hand qualitative research: understanding phenomena and developing hypotheses; the approach is open and interpretive.
4. **Empirical research process.** It starts with a question or problem. Step 1: an idea or assumption, and you formulate hypotheses. Step 2: literature research, clarifying what is already known. Step 3: planning and preparation. Step 4: collecting and analysing data. Step 5: writing up the results. Step 6: presenting and discussing them. The discussion raises new questions and the cycle starts again. With your six-pager you are between step 2 and step 3: literature is done, now you plan the approach.
5. **Friedrichs.** The German sociologist Jürgen Friedrichs described the logic behind this. He divides the process into three contexts: discovery (what should be researched?), justification (how should it be researched? the largest of the three) and utilisation (what happens with the results?). Friedrichs follows Karl Popper's critical rationalism: beliefs are not simply confirmed but repeatedly tested; you actively try to refute them. The famous example is swans: no matter how many white swans you see, "all swans are white" only holds until a non-white one appears. Hypotheses can never be finally proven, only provisionally supported or refuted. We now go through the three contexts one by one.
6. **Context of discovery.** It answers the "what": the reason that leads to a research project. Three typical triggers: a social or practical problem (e.g. skills shortage, digitalisation), a theory (e.g. two theories contradict each other), or a commission (e.g. from a ministry or company). From this the problem is sharpened, helped by exploration (a first look at the field, talking to practitioners and researchers); problem and exploration influence each other. Often the most creative phase, shaped by the researchers' interest and available resources. You already know this phase: when you found and narrowed your topic.
7. **Context of justification (1).** The largest area. It starts with the problem, theory and existing studies, from which hypotheses are developed. A research hypothesis is a statement based on theories or earlier findings, e.g. "Resource-management strategies promote students' learning success". In contrast, a research question in the narrower sense is a general question you ask when solid prior knowledge is missing, e.g. "Which learning and working strategies help students succeed?" Two directions follow: hypothesis-testing research (result: provisionally confirmed or refuted) and hypothesis-generating, exploratory research. For your six-pager: look at your literature. Is there enough prior knowledge for a justified hypothesis, or is your question more open and exploratory?
8. **Context of justification (2).** The slide shows the context of justification as a flow. Problem, theory and existing studies at the top, then two connected strands. Left, methodological: from hypotheses to a suitable method, sample and statistical procedures (coming shortly). Right, conceptual (the topic now): defining all terms used in a hypothesis, isolating the relevant variables, then operationalisation.
9. **Operationalisation.** Its purpose is to make abstract concepts measurable, including coding and building indicators. It links the conceptual level with observable reality. Simple example: satisfaction, measured on a scale from one (very dissatisfied) to five (very satisfied).
10. **Correspondence problem.** The key question: does the chosen indicator really measure the construct you want to study? Example: employee loyalty. You could measure intention to leave, feeling of attachment, or length of employment. So one construct can be measured with three different indicators, and depending on which you choose, you can get different results.
11. **Context of utilisation.** What happens with the results? First, discuss their contribution to solving the original problem; then practical application or at least recommendations; finally presentation in publications and talks. Three audiences: the scientific community, possibly the client, and interested practitioners. This is called science communication. The circle closes: results flow back into theory and lead to new questions.
12. **Methods map.** There are various methods for collecting data: surveys (attitudes, opinions, experiences), observation (directly capturing actions or processes), experiments (testing effects under controlled conditions), content analysis (systematically analysing texts, images or videos) and interviews (understanding experiences and interpretations in depth). Thank you for your attention, see you in the next learning video!
